# The comedy explainer formula ("laugh, then learn")

A reusable recipe for funny **and** informative short videos (45–60 s, 9:16), built on the **TikTok Retention
Rules** in `CLAUDE.md`. The retention rules decide *when* something happens; this formula decides *how it is funny*.
First used for `USMidterms` ("The president just failed his midterm.", `briefs/us-midterm-elections/`), which is the
worked example for every step below. Researched and written 4 Nov 2026.

How to ask for it: put **"funny"**, **"comedic"** or **"entertaining"** in the prompt (e.g. *"Make a funny motion
graphics video about how Japan's emperor is chosen"*). The agent then follows this file on top of the standing brief.

## 1. What the research says (why this works)

| Finding | What it means for our videos | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Humor **related to the content** goes with more learning; unrelated or put-down humor does not (Instructional Humor Processing Theory, 378 students). Retention of concepts taught with related humor was better after 6 weeks. | Every joke must be *about the fact itself*. A random joke is wasted time. | [Wanzer, Frymier & Irwin 2010, *Communication Education*](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/03634520903367238); [Edutopia summary](https://www.edutopia.org/blog/laughter-learning-humor-boosts-retention-sarah-henderson) |
| **Seductive details**: fun but irrelevant extras *hurt* recall, because they disturb how people organise the main ideas; about two thirds of the studies in a meta-analysis found the effect fully or partly, and it weakens when the fun part is relevant to the main idea. | Cut any gag that does not carry information, however funny. | [Harp & Mayer 1998 (summary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seductive_details); [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/20-1) |
| The **humor effect**: funny sentences are remembered better than plain ones, especially when mixed with plain ones. | Put the joke *on the key fact*, and keep the rest plain, so the key fact stands out. | [Schmidt 1994, *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition*](https://doi.org/10.3758%2FBF03194924) |
| Comedy can teach as well as the news: Colbert's Super PAC segments taught viewers more about campaign finance than CNN, Fox or MSNBC did (1,232 adults), thanks to **a story he took part in, plus satire**. John Oliver's net-neutrality segment produced the same knowledge gain as ABC News, but news made the issue feel **more important**. | Use one running story/premise, and keep **one sincere "why it matters" beat**, said plainly. | [Hardy et al. 2014, Annenberg](https://www.annenbergpublicpolicycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/Stephen-Colberts-Civics-Lesson_release_6-02-14_Final.pdf); [Becker & Bode 2018, *Information, Communication & Society*](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369118X.2017.1301517) |
| **Benign violation theory**: we laugh when something is "wrong" (breaks a norm) *and* safe at the same time; distance and a harmless target make it safe. | The "wrong" is a real quirk of the system; it is safe because it is true, historical, and nobody is mocked. | [McGraw & Warren 2010, *Psychological Science*](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf) |
| On TikTok, **humor and imitation predict likes, shares, comments and views** (1,025 science videos, 113 creators). But a clip that teases and then stays vague pushes people away. | Be funny *and* concrete: every tease gets a real answer (rule 2). | [Bunquin & Segrè Cohen 2026, *Public Understanding of Science* (via Univ. of Oregon)](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/482e9c19-7637-43a0-8fdd-cb88e810fa91/full); [Cacciatore & Yeo 2026 (Newswise)](https://www.newswise.com/articles/how-social-media-can-get-people-hooked-on-science) |
| Humor makes the speaker **more likeable without losing credibility**, but too much sarcasm or negativity, or offensive humor, costs credibility. Four decades of classroom studies agree: humor builds attention and a good mood; put-downs never help. | Warm, dry, deadpan; never sneering. | [Yeo et al. 2020 (summary)](https://www.newswise.com/articles/using-humor-in-communication-helps-scientists-connect-build-trust); [Banas et al. 2011 (Faculty Focus summary)](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/effective-teaching-strategies/humor-in-the-classroom-40-years-of-research/) |

**In one sentence:** the joke is the delivery van, the fact is the parcel. No parcel, no van.

## 2. The seven house rules

1. **The punchline is the fact.** Each joke states something true that the viewer should remember. *Delete test:*
   if you can cut the joke and lose no information, cut it.
2. **One comic premise for the whole video** (comedians call it "the game"). Pick one everyday thing the topic
   secretly resembles, and play it from the first frame to the last. *Midterms → a school exam the president sits;
   voters grade him by electing Congress.* Every section is a new move in the same game (the graders, the exam
   paper, the grade, the excuse), so the structure is easy to follow.
3. **Punch at the system, never at people.** Jokes target rules, history, numbers and institutions; never voters, a
   party, a group, or anyone's looks. *Both-sides test:* the joke must land the same whoever you vote for. This
   keeps the video neutral, as the standing brief requires.
4. **Straight setup, twist on the cut.** Say the setup as a plain fact; the twist lands on a hard cut, with a visual
   gag (stamp, sign, prop) and a short sound effect. The narrator stays deadpan: a calm British voice describing
   American chaos is a joke in itself.
5. **Exaggerate only what is literally true.** "The whole House reapplies for its job" is a cheeky way to say all 435
   seats are elected every two years. Qualify where needed ("almost always", "since World War Two"); the fact
   sheet holds the exact version.
6. **Plant, repeat, pay off.** Plant a prop or line in the first 5 s, bring it back 1–2 times, and pay it off at the end.
   The rewatch detail (rule 10) is the best plant: a small odd thing in frame 0 (*a horse sticker on the report card*)
   explained only at the end (*why Tuesday? horses*).
7. **One sincere beat.** Once, say plainly why it matters (*"From January, every law needs both parties."*). Comedy
   teaches facts; the sincere beat tells people the facts matter.

**Rhythm:** about one laugh every 5–8 s, at most one joke per beat, and **no joke inside the hardest explanation**
(the ballot and the seat maths stay clean; the button comes right after).

## 3. The toolbox (devices that carry facts)

| Device | Pattern | Example (US midterms) |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal pun on the topic's name** | Take the name literally | "Midterm" → the president sits an exam |
| **Everyday analogy** | "[System] is basically [everyday thing]" | Congress election = the grading |
| **Paradox hook** | "[Person] wasn't in it. [Person] lost anyway." | (A/B hook) "He wasn't on the ballot. He lost anyway." |
| **Deadpan number** | One small number, said flatly | "Since World War Two, only two presidents passed." |
| **Absurd reframe** | Describe a normal rule as if it were bizarre | "The whole House reapplies for its job." |
| **Personification** | A state, an office or a rule acts like a person | "North Dakota skips that." |
| **Understatement / escalation** | List, then the odd one out | "Americans even elect their sheriffs." |
| **Rule of three, twist last** | A, B, then the surprise (or the two-step version) | "Angry voters show up. Happy ones stay on the couch." |
| **Comic button** | Two words after a serious line | "Hello, gridlock." |
| **Callback** | Bring back frame 0's prop with a new meaning | The report card returns with the real result |
| **Absurd origin story** | Why is it like this? Because of something tiny and old | "In 1845, farmers rode to vote, never on Sundays. Hence, Tuesday." |
| **Visual gags** | Props react: stamps, signs, a ballot that keeps going | F stamps, a NOW HIRING sign, a sheriff's star, a couch |

## 4. The general script (fill in the blanks)

Beat by beat, with the retention rule each beat serves. Times assume about 165 spoken words at Kokoro speed 1.2;
measure and trim as usual. Lines in *italics* are patterns to adapt, not words to copy.

| # | Time | Retention job | Comedy job | Pattern (fill in) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–2 s | Hook (rule 1): result first, huge, complete on frame 0 | The premise as one punchline; plant the hidden prop | *"[Person/institution] just [did X as if it were an everyday thing]."* |
| 2 | 2–5 s | 3 open loops (rule 2) | Short questions, like a stand-up setup | *"What [X]? Who [Y]? Why [almost always Z]?"* |
| 3 | 5–10 s | Loop 1 partly answered | Map the premise onto the system | *"[When/where], [the real mechanism]. [Rule of the game], and [outcome]."* |
| 4 | 10–14 s | First surprising payoff (rule 3) | Deadpan number | *"Since [date], only [small number] [have done it]."* |
| 5 | ~14 s | Re-hook 1 on a full flash (rule 5) | Absurd reframe | *"But here's the weird part: [true rule said as if it were mad]."* |
| 6 | 17–25 s | The facts behind the re-hook; what each institution does | Contrast or button | *"[Numbers]. [Contrast]." / "Together, they [what they do]."* |
| 7 | 25–29 s | Who can vote (standing brief, ≤ 3–4 s) | One personified quirk | *"The [premise word for voters]: [who can vote]. [Place] [quirk]."* |
| 8 | ~29 s | Re-hook 2 on a full flash | Escalation | *"Now it gets strange: [weirdest true detail]."* |
| 9 | 32–39 s | The heart: the ballot and how votes become seats | **No jokes here**; keep it clean | *"[What the ballot looks like]. [How you fill it in]. [How votes become seats]."* |
| 10 | 39–42 s | The latest result | Callback to frame 0's prop | *"On [day], [result]."* |
| 11 | 42–46 s | The sincere beat | Comic button after it | *"From [date], [real consequence]. [Two-word button]."* |
| 12 | 46–49 s | Close the big "why" loop | Rule of three / two-step | *"Why? [Reason], [reason], [reason with a twist]."* |
| 13 | 49–51 s | Share reason (rule 9) | Gentle tease of the viewer's friend | *"Send this to someone who [relatable thing]."* |
| 14 | 51–57 s | Rewatch payoff (rule 10) | Absurd origin story | *"And the [planted prop]? [Tiny old reason]. Hence, [today's rule]."* |
| 15 | 57–60 s | YES/NO question in the hook's layout; hold 1–2 s (rules 8–9) | The last button, which loops into frame 0 | *"So, should [country] [change the thing the hook was about]?"* |

**Finding the material (do this before writing):**
1. Pick the **premise**: "[Topic] is basically [everyday thing]." Good premises have roles (who is graded, who grades,
   what the grade is) that map onto the real system.
2. List **five weird but true facts** and rank them by surprise: #1 → the hook, #2 → the first payoff, #3 → re-hook 1,
   #4 → re-hook 2, #5 → the rewatch detail (it must be something you can *show* small in frame 0).
3. For each fact write the **plain setup** (what it is) and the **twist** (why it is odd). Keep the twist under 6 words.
4. Run the delete test, the both-sides test and the "is it literally true?" test on every joke.
5. Write the lines straight into `src/voiceover/<name>.script.json` (no separate word-for-word script, as usual).

## 5. Comedy checks (score them next to the retention rules)

Score each out of 10 in `retention-plan.md`, after the eleven retention rules; fix anything below 7.

| Check | Passes when |
|---|---|
| C1 The joke is the fact | Every joke survives the delete test |
| C2 One premise | The premise is set by 10 s and called back at the end |
| C3 Neutral | Every joke passes the both-sides test; no person or group is the target |
| C4 Rhythm | A laugh or visual gag about every 5–8 s; none inside the ballot or seat maths |
| C5 Sincere beat | One plain "why it matters" line |
| C6 True | Every exaggeration is literally true or qualified; the fact sheet has the exact numbers |

**Visual comedy in this look:** gags are props and stamps (no tiny jokey text, rule 6), the re-hooks are full flashes
in the second flag colour, and each visual punchline lands on its word with a `pop`/`thud` sound effect.
